How to read brake and throttle traces

The speed graph shows what the car did. The brake and throttle graphs show what you did to make it happen. Read together, they answer most questions about a lap without any more advanced analysis — and a handful of shapes account for nearly everything a new driver can improve.

Start with speed

Plotted against distance, a lap's speed is a row of peaks and valleys. Each valley is a corner; each peak is the end of a straight. Three things to read from each corner:

  • Where the speed starts to fall — your braking point.
  • The lowest speed — your minimum corner speed, and where on the track it happens.
  • How steeply it climbs afterward — your exit.

A sharp V means braking, then straight back to power. A valley with a flat floor means time spent at a steady speed in the corner, neither slowing nor accelerating.

The brake trace

A well-judged braking zone has a recognizable shape: a fast rise to the hardest braking, held briefly, then a gradual release that tapers off as the car turns in. Hardest at the start, when the car is fastest; lightest at the end, when the tires are needed for cornering.

What you seeWhat it means
A slow ramp up to peak pressure Easing onto the brake. The car covers ground at full speed while braking builds, so the braking zone has to start earlier.
A low, long plateau Braking earlier and more gently than the car can manage.
Peak pressure, then an instant drop to zero Jumping off the brake. The nose rises as you turn in, which invites understeer.
Two separate humps in one braking zone Braking, releasing, then braking again: the first application was too early or too hard, or the braking point is uncertain.
A different shape every lap No fixed reference for where to brake.

The throttle trace

The ideal is one application: off the brake, a brief pause or a light steady throttle through the middle of the corner, then a smooth progression to full throttle that stays there until the next braking point.

  • A dip after you have started accelerating means the throttle went on before the car could take it, and you had to lift. Starting a little later and committing is faster.
  • A sawtooth through a corner is hunting for grip with your foot.
  • A long stretch short of full throttle on a straight is either caution or a habit. Check the speed trace: if the car was not near its limit, the straight is free time.
  • A lift at the end of a straight before the brake comes on is coasting.

Coasting

Look for stretches where both traces are at zero. The car is neither slowing as fast as it could nor accelerating, so it is losing time on both sides. Short gaps while moving your foot are normal; a gap of a car length or more before a braking zone, or a long neutral stretch in the middle of a corner, is the most common thing to find in a first season's data.

The opposite, both traces above zero together, is the two pedals overlapping. Unless you are doing it on purpose, it wears the brakes and slows the car.

Putting it to use

  1. Pick your fastest lap and one typical lap.
  2. Find the corner where the two differ most — a lap delta points straight at it.
  3. Compare the brake and throttle shapes in that corner only.
  4. Choose one thing to change next session, and look again afterward.

Review data in the paddock, and change braking points a little at a time, with room to spare.

Speed, brake and throttle in Track Evolution

When you import a recording, the session's channel graphs put its laps on one distance axis, with up to three laps overlaid in different colors. The Traces tab stacks speed, throttle and brake directly above one another, so what your feet did sits under what the speed did. Point at a graph (or tap it) to mark that place on every graph and on the track map.

Which graphs you get depends on the recording. Speed comes from GPS, so every source has it, including laps recorded with your phone and GoPro footage. Throttle and brake come from the car, so they need a recording that carries car data: a Corvette or Cadillac PDR video, a Porsche Track Precision export, or a VBO file with those channels logged.

The speed, throttle and brake graphs are free. The other channels, the delta chart and sector splits are part of Track Evolution Pro.
