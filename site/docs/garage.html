Garage & setup notebook

The paper notebook and the parts spreadsheet, folded into your logbook: track what's on the car and what it's set to, and let your logged events tell you when consumables will need replacing and what a setup change did to your times.

Keeping your cars — every vehicle, its notes, the default that fills in new events, and each car's track days and best laps — is free. Consumable wear tracking, what the car has cost and the setup notebook are part of Track Evolution Pro ($1.99/month or $19.99/year, one subscription across the iPhone, Android and web apps). See Free and Pro.

Vehicles and their garage pages

Your garage has its own page: on the web, the Garage link beside Events at the top of every page. It shows a card for each car — how many track days it has done and where it was last out — and an + Add car card that adds one, picked from the catalog or typed in. In the iPhone and iPad app it is the Garage tab (on Android, cars are under Settings → Vehicles for now). When an event's Car field matches a garage vehicle by name, the event links to that vehicle — that link is what lets parts accrue wear and setups carry over between events. Each vehicle gets a garage page with its track days, where it was last out and next going, its best lap at each track, and an Edit car button for its name, modifications and notes — and, with Pro, its track hours, consumables and spend. A vehicle can also carry a target hot tyre pressure — the pressure you want the tyres to reach on track — which the setup notebook's pressure loop (below) aims at; set it from Edit car or straight from an imported session's Car tab.

Edit car also takes two numbers off the car's spec sheet: its wheelbase (in millimetres) and its steering ratio (16.25 for "16.25:1"). They are what the balance read-out on an imported session needs to say how much a corner understeers, rather than only which corner behaves differently from the rest. Both are optional — leave them blank and the read-out keeps its relative reading — and both are in the owner's manual or the manufacturer's technical data. For the cars a track day usually sees, Track Evolution keeps a catalog of these figures, one entry per generation, and you never have to know them: the vehicle form has a Find your car in the catalog field — type a few letters ("c7", "corvette", "mx-5 nd") and pick your generation from the list, which shows the years so seven Corvettes don't read as one. Picking fills in both numbers and, if the car has no name yet, names it; a car you already named keeps its name. The numbers stay editable underneath, with a line saying where the catalog got them. If you had already typed a number, a pick asks before replacing it — each number separately, so a ratio you corrected to what your aftermarket rack really is survives. Picking a different car swaps the catalog's numbers for the new car's, and clearing the pick keeps the numbers as yours. A steering ratio is only ever filled in where the manufacturer quotes a single figure — a variable-ratio rack is left for you to decide, never guessed. The catalog is small and cached, so searching it works offline; saving the car, like every garage change, needs a connection.

For a car the catalog doesn't cover — or to check a number you typed — Track Evolution can measure the steering ratio from your own laps. Once the car has a wheelbase and a few imported sessions that recorded steering angle and yaw rate (a Corvette PDR recording does), a line appears under the steering-ratio field: "Measured from 6 sessions: 15.8:1", with a Use this button that fills the field in. The number comes from how much the car rotated for the steering it was given across a range of speeds, which is the same physics the balance read-out runs on, pooled across your most recent sessions so one damp day doesn't set it. If the field already holds a number, the same line becomes the sanity check — "— matches", or "— your 12:1 is well off this; check the units" — and a car whose rack changes ratio with speed says so rather than pretending to one figure. When there is nothing to measure from yet, the line simply isn't there.

Track hours — the wear clock

Everything wear-related runs on on-track hours, counted automatically from your events: 2 hours per track day by default, which is a realistic amount of green-flag seat time for an HPDE day. If a day ran long or short, set the exact hours on the event's edit form ("On-track hours") and that event uses your number instead. If you log complete lap data, an event's logged driving time is used when it exceeds the estimate.

Consumables and wear projections

Add parts — brake pads, tires, rotors, brake fluid, oil — to a vehicle with their install date, cost, and optionally an expected life in track hours. From then on wear accrues by itself as you log events. Each part shows a wear bar and a projection of what's left, from the best information available:

  • Measured — log a quick pad-thickness or tread-depth measurement between events (two or more), and the app fits your actual wear rate per track hour and projects when you'll hit the replace-at value. This is the number to trust.
  • Expected life — with no measurements, remaining life is expected hours minus accrued hours. When you retire a set and add its replacement, the replacement's expected life defaults to what the old set actually lasted.

When you swap a worn set for a fresh one of the same thing, use the part's Refresh button: it retires the current set (its history and measurements stay in the retired list) and starts a new one with the same details, hours reset to zero. The new set's expected life is recalibrated from what your retired sets actually lasted. If the new set differs — different compound, different price — refresh anyway and tweak it with Edit.

Parts due (or nearly due) for replacement surface as a maintenance-due strip on your dashboard — "front pads, about 1.5 track days left" before your next event, not after it. Tires also count heat cycles (one per event day), and retired parts keep their history: hours lived, cost, and cost per track hour.

The car's page also totals what it has cost: every part ever fitted, retired sets included, plus whatever its past track days cost if you enter that on your events (see what a day cost). Both are shown on the Spent tile, with the split underneath.

Your season's Wrapped names a favourite tyre (Pro) from the same records: the set of tires with the most track days on it that year, counting the events on its car between its install and retire dates — the same days its wear is counted from. Ties go to the one with more track hours. An event only counts toward a car when its car field matches a car in your garage.

The setup notebook

Every event carries one setup sheet per day: tire pressures (cold and hot, all four corners), camber, toe, caster, damper clicks, sway-bar settings, fuel, and which pad and tire sets were on the car. Sheets copy forward — a new sheet pre-fills from your previous day (or the car's last event), so you only edit what changed. Values that changed from the previous day are highlighted.

The sheet's cold pressures also close a loop with telemetry import: a Corvette PDR recording knows the hot pressures the tyres actually reached, so an imported session's Car tab lines up the cold pressures you set, the hot pressures the import measured and your car's target hot pressure, and says what cold pressure to start from next time — "LF ran 27.0 cold → 31.3 hot; start from 25.5". One button records the hot pressures on the day's sheet, along with a next time note that copies forward into your next sheet; on a multi-day event another opens the next day's sheet with the suggested cold pressures already in place. This half is web-only, like the notebook itself.

Setup vs. lap times

Each track page ends with a Setup vs. lap times table: every sheet you've logged at that track in date order, what changed between sheets, and the event's best time and consistency alongside. The app deliberately doesn't claim causation — conditions, traffic and driver all move lap times — but it does the recall perfectly, which is the part a paper notebook is bad at.

Private by design

Garage data — parts, wear, spend, and every setup sheet — is never included in share pages. Share your pace; your damper clicks stay yours.
