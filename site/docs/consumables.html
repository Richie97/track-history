Tracking brake pads, tires and fluids on track

A track day uses up brake pads, tires and fluid many times faster than the road does, and the usual way to find out is at the track, with a session left to run and nothing left on the pads. Tracking consumables is how you find out the week before instead. This page covers which clock to count wear on, what to measure, how to turn two measurements into a prediction, and what your parts really cost.

Brakes and tires are what keep the car on the track. The part maker's limits and your event's tech inspection come first, and if a part looks marginal, replace it. Nothing here is a reason to run a part longer than you otherwise would.

Count track time, not miles

"These pads last 30,000 miles" tells you nothing about a track day. An hour of lapping can take more out of a set of pads than a year of commuting, and a car that does four track days a year may wear its parts almost entirely in those few hours. The odometer barely moves.

The clocks that work are:

  • Track hours — time actually spent lapping. A typical HPDE day is four sessions of about 20 minutes, a little over an hour of running. This is the best single clock for pads, rotors, tires and oil.
  • Track days — simpler to count, and close enough when your days are all about the same length.
  • Heat cycles — each time a tire is brought up to temperature and cooled again. Track-oriented tires lose grip with heat cycles even when plenty of tread is left, so a tire can be finished by cycles before it is finished by wear.
  • The calendar — for brake fluid, which takes on moisture with age whether or not the car is driven.

What to track

PartWhat to measure or countSigns it is due
Brake pads Friction material thickness, inner and outer pad, each corner At or near the pad maker's minimum; one pad of a pair much thinner than the other; cracked or crumbling material
Rotors Thickness against the minimum stamped on the rotor; track hours Cracks reaching the edge or joining drilled holes, a deep lip at the outer edge, vibration under braking
Brake fluid Age since the last flush; track days since the last bleed A pedal that goes long or soft during a session; dark fluid; older than your event allows
Tires Tread depth across the tread; heat cycles; age from the date code on the sidewall At the wear bars; cords or uneven wear on a shoulder; grip that has gone away with tread still left
Engine oil Track hours since the last change Past the interval your manufacturer gives for track use, which is usually much shorter than the road interval

Many organizers set their own limits — a minimum pad thickness, brake fluid changed within a set number of months — on the tech inspection form. Read yours before the event, not at the gate.

Measuring

  • Pads. A pad thickness gauge reads the friction material through the wheel spokes or the caliper window, without taking anything apart. Measure the inner and the outer pad: the inner one often wears faster and is the one you cannot see.
  • Tires. A tread depth gauge in the same grooves each time, at the inside, middle and outside of the tread. On track the outside shoulder usually goes first, and an average hides it.
  • Be consistent. Same gauge, same spots, parts cold, and write the number down with the date. A measurement you cannot compare with the last one is not much use.

Once before the event and once after is enough. It takes five minutes with the wheels on.

From two measurements to a prediction

One measurement tells you where a part is. Two, with the track time between them, tell you how fast it is going — and that gives you a date.

An example. A set of front pads starts with 10 mm of material. After four track days — five hours of lapping — it measures 7.5 mm:

  • Wear rate: 2.5 mm in 5 hours, or 0.5 mm per track hour.
  • You plan to replace them at 3 mm, so there is 4.5 mm left to use.
  • 4.5 mm at 0.5 mm an hour is 9 more track hours — about seven days of the same length.

So this set will not need changing for the next event or the one after, and you know roughly when to have the next set on the shelf. Every new measurement sharpens the estimate.

With no measurements yet, the fallback is history: if your last set of the same pads lasted 14 track hours, expect about that from this one.

Why the rate moves

A wear rate belongs to one car, one compound and one way of driving it. Expect it to change when any of these do:

  • The circuit. A track with several heavy braking zones can use pads twice as fast as a flowing one.
  • Your pace. As you get quicker you brake later and harder. Wear per hour goes up as lap times come down.
  • The compound. A track pad, a street pad and a different tire all have their own rate. Start the count again when you change.
  • The weather. Hot days are harder on tires and fluid.

That is the reason to keep measuring instead of trusting a number from a forum, or from your own first season.

What a part really costs

Divide what a set cost by the track hours it lasted and you have its cost per track hour. A $300 set of pads that lasts 14 hours costs about $21 an hour; a $180 set that lasts 6 costs $30. The cheaper set is the more expensive one. The same sum for tires is often the largest running cost of a track day after the entry fee, and it is the honest way to compare a compound that grips for longer against one that is cheaper to buy.

More than one set

Many drivers end up swapping parts for the track: a second set of wheels and tires, track pads fitted for the weekend and street pads put back afterward, wet-weather tires that come out twice a year. Each set then has its own life, and only the days it was actually on the car should count against it. Keep the count per set, not per car, or the numbers stop meaning anything the first time you swap.

Tracking parts in Track Evolution

Track Evolution's garage does this bookkeeping from the events you already log. Add a part to a car — pads, tires, rotors, brake fluid, oil — with what it cost and, if you like, how many track hours you expect from it. From then on its hours count up on their own: 1 hour 15 minutes per track day by default, or the total of your logged lap times once an event has three or more laps.

  • Wear projections. Log a pad thickness or tread depth between events and, from the second measurement on, the app works out your wear rate per track hour and how much life is left, in hours and in track days. Without measurements it uses the expected life, which defaults to what your previous set of the same part lasted.
  • Due before the weekend. The Garage and each car's page lead with the parts that are due or close to it, and your next event names the most urgent part on the car you are taking.
  • Spares and swaps. Every part has an Equipped switch. A part on the shelf stops accruing wear, and a set swapped on in the middle of a track day gets only the sessions it ran.
  • Heat cycles. Tires count one per event day they were on the car.
  • Cost per track hour. A retired part keeps its hours, its cost and the cost per hour, and the car's page totals what it has cost you.

Keeping your cars in the garage is free. Consumable wear tracking, projections and costs are part of Track Evolution Pro, on the web and in the iPhone and Android apps.
