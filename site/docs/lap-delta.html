How to find time with a lap delta

Two lap times tell you one lap was four tenths quicker. A lap delta tells you where those four tenths came from. It is the single most useful graph in data analysis, and it needs nothing more than the speed of two laps — which any GPS recording has.

What a delta is

A delta graph compares two laps at the same place on the track. Line both laps up by distance from the start/finish line, and at every point ask: how far ahead or behind is this lap compared with the reference lap? Plot that gap all the way round and you have the delta.

It starts at zero at the line and ends at the difference between the two lap times. Everything interesting is in how it gets there.

Read the slope, not the height

The most common mistake is to look at how high the line is. The height only says how much time has built up so far. What matters is whether the line is moving:

  • Flat — the two laps are going the same speed here. Nothing is being gained or lost, however far apart they already are.
  • Climbing — this lap is losing time to the reference right now.
  • Falling — this lap is gaining it back.
  • A step — a short, sharp change is one event: a missed braking point, a slide, a lift for traffic.
  • A long ramp — a steady climb down a straight is an exit problem. The car left the corner slower and pays for it every metre until the next braking zone.

Tracing a loss back to its cause

Time shows up on the delta after the mistake that caused it, so look backward from where the line starts to move:

  1. Find the biggest climb. Ignore the small wiggles; one or two places usually account for most of the gap.
  2. Find where it begins. That point, or just before it, is where the two laps started to differ.
  3. Look at the speed trace there. Did the slower lap brake earlier, carry less speed at the slowest point of the corner, or get back to full throttle later?
  4. Check what happened next. A lap that brakes later and gains into a corner often gives it all back, and more, on the way out. The delta shows the net result, which is the only one that counts.

As a rule, time lost on corner exit is the most expensive, because it carries down the whole of the following straight. Slow corners before long straights are where to look first.

Which laps to compare

  • Your best lap against your second best. Same car, same day, same tires. The differences are all yours, and all repeatable.
  • Today's best against your all-time best at the track, to see what has changed since.
  • A typical lap against your best. This shows which corners you only get right occasionally — see lap time consistency.

Comparing against another driver in another car is interesting but harder to act on: power, tires and weight explain much of the difference. Start with yourself.

Where a delta misleads

  • Traffic. A lap spent behind another car shows losses that have nothing to do with your driving. Compare clear laps.
  • Different lines. A lap that runs wider covers more distance, which shifts where things appear on a distance axis by a few metres.
  • GPS precision. From a phone, read the delta for the big features, not for hundredths.

Lap deltas in Track Evolution

Any session with telemetry — an imported video or data file, or laps recorded with your phone — has channel graphs with its laps as selectable chips. Select two or three laps and a delta chart appears above the speed trace, measured against the fastest of the laps you picked: where the line climbs, time is slipping away. Point at the place (or tap it) and it is marked on every graph below and on the track map, so the corner is one glance away.

To compare across days, a track's page has Compare two laps, which opens on your latest event's best lap against your all-time best there, with the delta, both laps' speed and inputs, and a head-to-head table. A Distance / Time switch re-plots the graphs against the clock, which shows the slower lap falling behind on the map.

In the phone apps, a mounted phone also shows a live delta to your best lap of the session while recording — green when you are up, red when you are down. It is for a glance on a straight, never for study while driving.

The delta chart and the two-lap compare are part of Track Evolution Pro, as is recording with the phone. Importing a file and its speed graph are free.
