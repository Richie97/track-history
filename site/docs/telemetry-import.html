Telemetry import

On any event page, Import video / telemetry… turns recordings into sessions with laps. Parsing happens entirely on your own device — for videos, via byte-range reads of the embedded telemetry track (a few MB of a multi-GB file). Files never leave your computer or phone.

Importing is free — the lap times, the racing line and the car's top speed, max RPM and max lateral G come out of any supported file at no cost. The per-lap channel graphs, sector splits and lap-vs-lap deltas inside that same file are part of Track Evolution Pro ($1.99/month or $19.99/year, one subscription across the iPhone, Android and web apps). See Free and Pro.

On your phone

Video imports in the iPhone and Android apps as well as in the browser: tap Import video on an event page and pick the clip from Files or Photos, or share it to Track Evolution (Open with on iPhone, Share on Android) from wherever the clip already is. It is the same import — the same lap times, the same racing line, the same channel graphs.

That is where the footage usually is first: a GoPro clip comes over Wi-Fi into Photos and a Corvette PDR clip lands in Files off a USB stick, both before the laptop is opened. The clip is read where it sits — nothing is copied and nothing is uploaded, which also means a multi-gigabyte video imports without needing gigabytes of free space.

VBO files stay on the web app. A VBOX writes to an SD card that gets read on a computer, so the browser is already where that file lands.

The start/finish line picker

GPS-only sources have no lap markers, so the import preview shows the track map you actually drove: click where the start/finish line is and laps are timed on each pass across it. Crossings are interpolated between 10–18 Hz GPS fixes, so times are accurate to roughly ±0.1–0.3 s and shown with a ~ prefix.

One picked line applies to every file in the batch, so a day's worth of sessions needs a single click. Racelogic's west-positive longitude convention is detected and mirrored automatically.

How PDR lap times are derived

The PDR logic is reverse-engineered from the recorder's telemetry track and validated against Cosworth Toolbox lap times:

  • PDR Beacon events mark start/finish crossings to the millisecond, but the recorder drops some crossings.
  • The cumulative odometer channel recovers the missing ones: beacon-to-beacon distance ÷ crossing count gives the lap length, and a missing crossing is the moment distance passes D0 + k × lapLength (accurate to ~0.05–0.3 s, shown with ~).
  • Crossings beyond the first/last beacon are extrapolated the same way and sanity-checked against GPS latitude.
  • The recording's GPS trace is decoded from the telemetry stream too, so a PDR file with no beacons gets the same track map and start/finish line picker as the other GPS sources. The import preview also reads the car channels and shows each session's top speed, max RPM and max lateral G, saved into the session notes.
  • A PDR recording also carries slow readings that change over a lap rather than through one — oil, coolant and transmission temperatures, oil pressure, fuel level, tyre pressures and temperatures, battery voltage — so those are kept as one figure per lap, along with the ambient temperature, the track's elevation range and the car's odometer reading for the session. The per-lap figures are the channel panel's Car tab (below); all of it is saved at import because the video stays on your device and is never uploaded, so a reading not taken now is gone.
  • Each imported session also stores per-lap channel data — speed for every source, plus, from a PDR recording, RPM, lateral and longitudinal G, throttle, brake, steering angle, yaw rate, gear, wheel slip, boost and whether ABS or traction control was active (whichever channels the recording carries — a channel the file lacks simply draws no graph) — sampled along the lap's driven distance. On the event page the session's lap chips double as the picker for the expandable channel graphs: laps overlaid on a shared distance axis, comparing up to three laps side by side. With two or more laps selected, a delta chart appears above the channels showing where time is gained or lost against the fastest of the selection — the trace climbing means time slipping away, so the corner that costs you shows up as the spot where it climbs. Above the charts, a sector table splits each highlighted lap into three sectors along its own driven distance — no setup needed — marking the session's best in each sector and the gap to it otherwise, and the best sectors summed give a theoretical best lap that also appears in the session's stats line when it beats your actual best: the gap is what stringing your best sectors together would have been worth. The panel is split into tabs — Time (delta, sectors, speed), Inputs (pedals, steering, RPM, gears), Grip (the friction circle, balance, lateral G and yaw rate) and Car (is the car okay, and is it set up right) — so each answers one question. From a PDR recording, a gear ribbon sits under the RPM trace — one band per selected lap, one block per gear with the number in it, a gap where the clutch was in — and with two or more laps selected the stretches where they were in different gears are outlined, so "third on my best lap, fourth on this one" is visible without reading a chart. Beside it, a shift points table gives your upshift RPM from each gear (earliest, typical and latest across the session) with a note when a gear is taken to the top of the day's rev range or shifted out of noticeably earlier than another; the typical upshift RPM also joins the session's stats line. A PDR recording's wheel-slip and ABS / traction / stability channels become marks on the best-lap track map rather than graphs: a shape wherever that lap hit ABS, locked a wheel, spun the driven wheels or had a system cut in, coloured by kind — braking-side events in one colour, power-side in another, each told apart by shape as well — with the same stretches shaded on the brake, throttle and steering traces for every selected lap, and a line in the session's stats counting the places where each happened, or "no interventions" when nothing fired (which is normal on track with the systems off). On the Grip tab, a PDR recording's cornering and braking/acceleration forces are plotted against each other as a friction circle: one point per 20 m of track for each selected lap, braking at the top, power at the bottom and cornering to the sides, with a dashed arc at the strongest combined grip of the session. A tyre has one grip budget and it can be spent in any direction — brake in a straight line, turn, then accelerate and your points draw a cross; trail the brake into the corner and feed the throttle out of it and they fill the circle. The space between the two is time. Under it, two numbers say how much of your tyre work was combined — braking while cornering, and cornering while on the power — for each selected lap and for the session, and they are worth watching across a day. On the web app you can also hover a point to see where on the lap it happened: the distance is marked on the other graphs and, for the lap the map was drawn from, the spot is ringed on the track map. Two things to know: left and right come from your steering, since the recording stores cornering force without a side; and 20 m samples smooth the spikes, so the circle shows the shape of your grip use rather than your peak G (that's the max lateral G in the session's stats). The circle and its two numbers are on the iPhone and Android apps too. The Car tab is the other half of a track day: every slow reading a PDR recording carries — oil, coolant and transmission temperature, oil pressure, boost, the four tyre pressures and temperatures, fuel and battery voltage — as one small chart per figure across the session's laps, each with the number that matters for it (the peak of a temperature, the lowest oil pressure or voltage, the tyre pressure as the lap ended), shaded when a figure approaches or passes a line worth watching, such as oil over about 130 °C or the battery under 12.5 V. Under the charts, the tyre spread per lap — left minus right on each axle and front minus rear, for temperatures and pressures — is the camber and balance evidence a setup change gets judged by, a fuel line gives your burn per lap and the laps left at that rate (it joins the session's stats line too), and a per-lap table carries every figure. A session typed in by hand has none of this and shows no Car tab. On the web app the tab also closes the loop with your setup notebook: the sheet's cold pressures, the hot pressures the import measured and your car's target hot pressure become the cold pressure to start from next time, recordable onto the day's sheet in one tap. Under it, the same tab answers am I understeering or oversteering? from the recording's yaw rate and steering angle. In a neutral car the two rise together: steering the car doesn't answer means the front is washing out — understeer — and rotation you didn't ask for means the rear is coming round — oversteer. Most of us believe we're oversteering when we're actually understeering, because understeer feels like nothing happening. The balance chart plots each selected lap's samples with steering across and how much the car rotated per metre up, with a dashed line for how this car typically responds: points above the line are oversteer, below it understeer. Under the chart, the lap is split into corners — stretches of sustained cornering force, numbered T1, T2… from the start/finish line (the app's numbering, not the circuit's) — and each corner gets a reading per selected lap and for the session: "T1 understeer 14%, T7 neutral, T10 slight oversteer 9%" is something you can take to the setup sheet, and the corners that stand out join the session's stats line. On the web app you can hover a point or a corner row to see where on the lap it is. One thing to know: the reading is relative. The exact version needs your car's wheelbase and steering ratio, which aren't recorded, so each corner is compared with how the car responded on average across the session — a car that pushes in every corner reads neutral in every corner, and what shows up is the corner that behaves differently from the rest. Balance is on the iPhone and Android apps too. (This is derived at import time; the recording itself is never uploaded, so re-import a file if you delete a session.)
  • Laps from different sessions and events compare too: the track page's Compare two laps puts any two laps with channel data side by side — it opens on your latest event's best lap against your all-time best — with a head-to-head table (lap time, top/min/average speed, throttle and braking shares), the pair's sector splits and combined theoretical best, the delta chart and the channel graphs on one distance axis. It's on the phone apps as well as the web, and works offline for laps you've viewed before. If the two laps cover clearly different driven distances (a different layout, or a different start/finish line between imports), the page says so rather than lining them up silently.
  • If a recording's GPS can't be decoded, a beacon-less file still gets lap times: latitude as a function of odometer distance repeats every lap, so the lap length is that profile's autocorrelation peak and laps are cut every lap-length of distance. Start/finish alignment comes from a beacon-timed recording of the same track imported in the same batch; without one, laps are still full laps of the track, just cut from where the car first reaches pace rather than at the official line.
Millisecond-exact times are shown as-is; anything derived by interpolation or odometer recovery carries a ~ so you always know which laps are measured and which are estimated.
