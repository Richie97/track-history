Your first track day

You don't need a race car, a racing license or any experience to drive your own car on a real race track. This page covers what a track day is, what you and the car need, where to find one, and what happens on the day — the things that are hard to find out until you've done one.

Every organizer runs things a little differently, and their rules are the ones that count — read the event's own requirements before you go. Track Evolution isn't affiliated with any of the organizations listed here.

What a track day is

Most first track days are an HPDE — High Performance Driver Education. It is not racing: there are no results, no prizes and no wheel-to-wheel driving. It's a day of instruction on a closed circuit, in your own car, with corner workers and safety crews on hand.

  • Run groups. Drivers are grouped by experience — usually three or four groups, from novice to advanced — and the groups take turns on track in sessions of about 20–30 minutes. Expect four to six sessions in a day.
  • An instructor. In the novice group an instructor normally rides in the passenger seat with you, or coaches you between sessions. Novices usually get classroom sessions too.
  • Passing is controlled. In the novice group you only pass on the straights the organizer names, and only when the car ahead signals you by — a "point-by". Nobody can force a pass.

What the car needs

The car you drive every day is very likely fine. What matters is that it's in good condition. Most organizers ask for a tech inspection — a short form you or a shop fill in before the event, sometimes checked again at the track. Expect it to cover:

  • Brakes — plenty of pad left (more than half is a good rule) and fresh brake fluid; a track day heats brakes far beyond anything the road does. A high-temperature fluid is a common first upgrade.
  • Tires — good tread, no cords or damage, and the correct pressures. Bring a gauge: pressures rise as the tires get hot.
  • Wheels — lug nuts torqued to spec.
  • Fluids — no leaks, oil and coolant topped up.
  • Battery — securely tied down.
  • Nothing loose — empty the cabin and the trunk. Floor mats, water bottles and phone mounts come out unless they're bolted in.

Check your insurance. Many street policies exclude track use, even at a non-competitive event. Separate track-day insurance exists if you want the car covered.

What you need

  • A helmet. Most organizers require a Snell SA-rated helmet within its approval window (check the event's rules for which ratings are accepted). Some loan or rent helmets to first timers — ask.
  • Clothing — long sleeves and long pants in a natural fiber like cotton, and closed shoes with thin soles.
  • A driver's license, and the signed waiver at registration.
  • For the day — water, food, sunscreen, a chair, a tire gauge, a torque wrench, a quart of oil, and painter's tape for a car number if the organizer uses them.

Finding an event

A single day usually costs a few hundred dollars, more at the bigger circuits. These are good places to start in the US:

WhereWhat it is
MotorsportReg The calendar most US clubs and organizers sign up through. Search near you for "HPDE" or "driver education" to see what's running at the tracks you can reach.
Track Night in America (SCCA) Weeknight evening events built for first-timers — among the cheapest ways onto a track, with a novice group and classroom sessions.
NASA HPDE The National Auto Sport Association's driver education program, run by regional chapters, with a structured path from novice up.
Hooked on Driving Track days across the country with one-on-one instruction in the novice group.
Chin Track Days Track days with driver coaching at major road courses.
Car clubs — PCA, BMW CCA and others Many marque clubs run excellent driver education days through their local chapters, with instructors in the car. Several welcome other makes too — check the event.

Many tracks also run their own open track days — look on the website of the circuit nearest you. Outside the US, track days are most often sold by the circuits themselves.

On the day

  1. Arrive early. Registration, tech and the drivers' meeting all happen before the first session, and the novice group is often first out.
  2. The drivers' meeting. This is where you learn the flags, the passing zones and the pit-lane rules for that track. Don't skip it.
  3. Your sessions. Start slow, learn the line from your instructor and build speed a little each session. Smoothness comes before speed.
  4. Cool-down lap. At the checkered flag, drive a slow lap with the brakes off as much as you can. In the paddock, don't set the parking brake on hot brakes — leave it in gear or chock a wheel.
  5. Between sessions — drink water, check tire pressures and look the car over.

Moving up

After a few events, an instructor signs you off to drive solo, and later to move up a run group. Organizers often ask about your experience when you register — how many days, at which tracks, what lap times. A share link to your Track Evolution history answers that in one link.

Logging it in Track Evolution

  • Add the event as soon as you've booked it — the track, the dates and the car. Its prep checklist starts with the items above, and you can edit the list to make it your own.
  • Record your laps with the phone's GPS in the iPhone or Android app — it stays in your pocket or a mount and needs no signal. See Recording laps.
  • Import video from a camera or a car's built-in recorder later, or type your lap times in by hand. See Telemetry import.
  • Share with your instructor — with Pro, they can read your logbook and see where the time went. See Share with a coach.
